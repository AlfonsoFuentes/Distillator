SCOPE
This section describes vaporizing equipment, provides design guidelines, and presents simplified hand calculation procedures
for the heat transfer and hydraulic balance of vaporizers. The calculation procedures are based on methods presented in the
Heat Transfer Research, Inc., (HTRI) Design Manual, Volumes 2 and 3, and Design Practices Section XIV, Fluid Flow. The
recommended design procedure is to use the appropriate computer program as described in Section IX-K. Tube selection,
circulation rate, and general design criteria for steam generators are also covered in this section. Sections IX-A, B, and C
describe basic vaporizer considerations such as tube size and pitch, fouling factors, etc. Section IX-A also includes a brief
description of enhanced boiling tubes that can be used to increase the nucleate boiling coefficient.

EQUIPMENT TYPES AND APPLICATIONS
REBOILER TYPES
General
Distillation column reboilers can be divided into three groups according to their method of fluid circulation:
1. Thermosiphon or natural circulation reboilers - The driving force is provided by the liquid head from the bottom of the
tower. The available head must be sufficient to overcome pressure drops in the inlet and outlet piping and in the
exchanger. The boiling fluid can be placed on the tubeside, or on the shell side of a conventional shell-and-tube
exchanger, or on the shell side of a kettle type exchanger.
2. Pump-through or forced circulation reboilers - A pump is used to circulate the boiling fluid through the exchanger and back
to the tower. The boiling fluid is generally placed on the tubeside of a conventional shell-and-tube exchanger .
3. Internal or stab-in reboilers - A tube bundle is inserted into the tower eliminating the need to provide for circulation of the
fluid.
Figure A1 shows sketches of different reboiler types and lists the advantages and disadvantages of each. The selection of the
type of unit is based on the heat transfer performance, fouling tendency, and the economics of the design; hydrodynamic
performance varies widely for the different types of exchangers, and is an important consideration. The economic factors
include foundation and piping costs, exchanger shell and bundle costs, tower elevation, and operating costs. The performance
factors are the rate of heat transfer and vaporization, pressure drop, and flow stability. The Reboiler Selection Chart below is a
guide to equipment selection. This is a rough guide and the ultimate selection depends on many more specific criteria.
Kettle Reboilers
The components of a typical kettle reboiler are shown in Figure A2. The bundles are normally two-pass, and U tubes if a clean
heating fluid such as steam is used, or a pull through floating head (T type) if the heating fluid is fouling. The bundle is inserted
in an enlarged shell to provide vapor-liquid disengaging space above the bundle. An overflow weir is normally provided to
maintain the liquid level above the top of the tubes.
Kettle reboilers may have 100% vaporization for clean fluids such as refrigerants. If the boiling fluid tends to foul then an
intermittent blowdown connection is provided at the bottom of the shell to remove fouling material accumulating at the bottom of
the shell.
With kettle reboilers the bottom section of the column is simplified. Product hold-up or vapor-liquid disengaging space is not
required in the tower, so internal baffling is minimized. If the necessary liquid hold-up beyond the weir in the exchanger
requires more than 4 ft of shell length a thermosiphon should be considered. Kettles require the lowest tower elevation of the
various reboiler types and so find application where the tower bottoms are not pumped away.

Vertical Thermosiphon Reboilers
These are usually single tube-pass units with vaporization in the tubes. Installation costs are low since the exchanger may be
directly mounted on the tower minimizing foundation, structures, and plot area; on the other hand, if independently supported in
the vertical orientation, these installation costs are probably the same as a horizontal unit or sometimes even higher. Bottoms
product hold-up and vapor-liquid disengaging space are provided in the tower bottom. Piping must be short but of large
diameter to reduce the pressure drop and ensure flow stability. The shell side fluid should be non-fouling so as to reduce the
cost of having to remove the bundle vertically and to permit fixed tubesheet construction.
Vertical thermosiphons should be restricted to a maximum of 50% vaporization because at higher rates of vaporization
hydrodynamic head losses may exceed the available driving force and cause unstable flow by creating temporary backflow of
vapor. It is common industrial practice to limit vaporization to 30%.
Flow instability can be prevented by increasing tube diameter, especially for low pressure services. Tubes of 3/4 in.
(19.05 mm) OD should be avoided if possible. Increasing tube length may promote instability by increasing pressure drop.
Rules of thumb to obtain a stable design are: (a) the pressure drop in the exit piping should be less than 0.3 times the total
driving head, (b) the flow area of the exit piping should be no less than the total flow area of the tubes, and (c) the flow area of
the inlet piping should be in the range of 0.1 to 0.3 times the total flow area of the tubes.
Detailed information on operational characteristics and design suggestions are given in Tables 1 and 2. Thermosiphons
should be avoided for viscous fluids and for operations where the available liquid head is variable.

Horizontal Thermosiphon Reboilers
The boiling fluid is placed on the shell side and multiple inlets and outlets, and split horizontal baffles are often used to provide
good distribution and higher velocities. Bottoms product hold-up and vapor-liquid disengaging are provided in the bottom of the
tower. This type is limited to moderately fouling services and the use of a removable bundle with square tube pitch is
recommended.
Horizontal thermosiphons require separate foundations, more plot area than vertical units, and more complex piping. Thus
their overall cost is generally higher than vertical thermosiphons.
Vaporization should be restricted to 50% for hydrocarbons and 10% for aqueous solutions for clean services and 25% for
relatively more fouling services. The advantage of horizontal thermosiphons is their lower pressure drop and larger liquid
volume hold-up than the vertical thermosiphons, that allows for greater operational flexibility and their lower head requirements.
Horizontal thermosiphons can handle fluid mixtures with wider boiling range; however, viscous services and those with variable
liquid head should be avoided.
Pump-Through Reboilers
In a pump-through reboiler the vaporizing fluid is circulated by a pump accommodating a higher pressure drop and
correspondingly higher heat transfer coefficients. Piping diameters may be smaller than for other reboiler types and the
exchanger can be located away from the tower. Initial pump costs and operating costs are high, but they can be justified under
the following circumstances:
1. The service is very viscous or highly fouling.
2. A high circulation rate, about 10:1, or a low increase in temperature is required to prevent thermal degradation of the
bottoms product.
3. The liquid head available from the tower is limited. However, tower elevation must be high enough to provide the required
NPSH.
In highly fouling services the vapor fraction at the outlet should be limited to about 5% by weight.
Internal Reboilers
An internal reboiler consists of a tube bundle inserted directly into the tower using a flange in the side of the tower. This
eliminates the costs of a shell and piping. The flange can be relatively expensive, but on an installed cost per square foot basis
internal reboilers are frequently the least expensive of all the types. A major limitation of this type is that the length of the
bundle is restricted by the tower diameter, so that an uneconomically large bundle diameter may be required. As a rule of
thumb, if the bundle diameter is 36 in. (914 mm) or greater, internal reboilers should not be considered. The major
disadvantage of internal reboilers is that they cannot be serviced and cleaned without shutting the tower down. For that reason
welded tube-to-tubesheet joints should be provided.
In sizing the bundle and the flange connection, sufficient clearance should be provided between the bundle and the flange neck
inside diameter to allow vapor to escape into the tower and avoid vapor blanketing of that portion of the tubes. Fouling
tendencies of internal reboilers are similar to kettles.
REBOILERS AND TOWER ELEVATION
The bottom of the shell of a reboiler is located at the minimum practical distance above grade. This distance is set by space
requirements for piping, and is usually about 3 ft (1 m).
For natural-circulation reboilers a pressure balance through the reboiler circuit determines the exact tower elevation. The usual
points of reference for the pressure balance are the bottom tangent line of the tower and the bottom of the reboiler shell or the
bottom tubesheet for a vertical thermosiphon reboiler. As a rough guide, the distance between these points will normally be 6
to 10 ft (1.8 to 3 m) for kettle reboilers and 8 to 20 ft (2.4 to 6.1 m) for thermosiphon reboilers.
For forced-circulation reboilers, the tower is normally elevated 15 ft (4.6 m) above grade to provide an adequate pump NPSH.
If necessary this elevation can be decreased by reducing the pump NPSH requirement to a minimum consistent with pump
availability. However, this will usually cause a considerable increase in the cost of the pump.

CHILLERS
The operation of a chiller is like that of a reboiler. The fluid being cooled on the tube side vaporizes refrigerant on the shell
side. A chiller is normally constructed like a kettle reboiler, but without a weir. It may also be made from a regular shell and
tube exchanger by eliminating tubes in the top portion of the shell to provide a space for vapor-liquid disengaging. The
calculation procedure for chillers is the same as for kettle reboilers.
PREHEATERS
A preheater, like a pump-through reboiler, consists of a conventional shell-and-tube exchanger with the liquid to be vaporized
fed either by a pump or pressurized from upstream equipment. Most preheaters add both latent heat and sensible heat to the
fluid being vaporized (one exception would be the vaporization of a pure component under very little back pressure). The fluid
to be vaporized is generally placed in the tubes to avoid potential phase separation that would occur if fluid is placed on the
shellside. Preheaters are most often used for feed streams to distillation columns. A lower level of heat can be used in tower
feed preheating rather than in reboilers. Also, tower loadings can be balanced by trading off between feed preheat and heat
input to the reboiler.
WASTE HEAT BOILERS
Applications
Waste heat boilers (steam generators) are commonly used to recover heat from catalytic cracker regenerator flue gas, thermal
reactor and condensers in a sulfur plant, reformer effluent, gas turbine exhaust, catalyst-oil slurries, and fixed bed reactors.
Types of Units
There are two types of units used for waste heat boilers (WHB): firetube or water tube. In firetube units (the more common
type) the heating fluid passes through the tubes and steam is generated outside the tubes (shell side).
Firetube Units
Firetube units usually come as thermosiphon or kettle type waste heat boilers. Generally, kettles are provided for
such services as hot product coolers (Resid, FCC Bottoms), or for Sulfur Condensers. Maximum heat flux normally
does not exceed 35,000 Btu/hr ft2 (110,000 W/m2) and maximum process inlet temperature is 800°F (430°C). Where
the maximum heat flux is greater than 40,000 Btu/hr ft2 (125,000 W/m2) and where the process inlet temperature is
greater than 900°F (480°C), a thermosiphon type WHB is generally recommended. In a thermosiphon type WHB,
steam is generated on the shellside of a tube bundle but the steam disengagement occurs in a separate shell, called
the Steam Drum. Flow between the WHB and the Steam Drum is maintained by natural circulation because of the
large density difference between the water “leg”, i.e., the downcomer, and the steam “leg”, i.e., the riser. The
circulation rate for thermosiphon-type WHB, is generally between 15 to 20:1, depending on the severity of the process
and the design maximum heat flux. Reliable designs for the WHB would take into account the possibility of vapor
blanketing at the hot end, where the maximum heat flux is encountered. WHB having a high inlet process
temperature, > 1000°F (535°C), are supplied with a protective refractory lining inside the inlet channel and on the
tubesheet; metallic or ceramic ferrules are supplied at each tube end.

Water Tube Units
Water tube units which generate steam inside the tubes are commonly used in flue gas exhaust from gas turbines, CO
boilers, FCC units, etc., resulting in a low gas pressure drop. Water-tube WHB are normally built into three sections:
a steam generator, a superheater and an economizer. For process streams with catalyst fines, i.e. FCC Gas Cooler,
maximum gas velocity across the tube bank should not exceed 100 fps; a deresonating baffle is also furnished to
prevent acoustic vibration; cleaning lanes are provided for steam lancing.
DESIGN CONSIDERATIONS
MAXIMUM AND DESIGN HEAT FLUX (FILM BOILING, VAPOR BLANKETING, CHOKE FLOW)
Pool Boiling
This type of boiling takes place when the heating surface is surrounded by a pool of liquid which is not flowing at any
appreciable velocity. The main boiling mechanism is the formation of bubbles and is known as nucleation. Kettles and internal
reboilers operate in pool boiling. The typical pool boiling curve, plotted as heat flux vs. the temperature driving force (∆T), is
shown in the figure below. As ∆T is increased heat transfer takes place by natural convection and eventually bubbles are
formed at the tube wall and are carried away by convection and buoyancy. This is known as nucleate boiling. Liquid fills in the
space vacated by the bubbles so that the tube wall always has liquid in contact with it. As ∆T is further increased (usually in the
range of 40 - 90°F for pure hydrocarbons, 22 - 50°C) liquid can no longer exist in contact with the metal surface. Bubbles are
formed faster than they can be removed and a vapor film blankets the tube surface. This is known as film boiling. The
transition region between nucleate boiling and stable film boiling consists of unstable nucleate and unstable film boiling. This
transition begins when the maximum heat flux possible in nucleate boiling, also called the critical heat flux, is reached.
Because heat transfer coefficients are highest in the nucleate boiling region for practical values of ∆T, the film boiling region is
avoided in design. To ensure operation in nucleate boiling the design heat flux should be limited to 70% of the calculated
maximum flux. If the design ∆T is such that film boiling cannot be avoided then the heat flux should be kept above the
minimum value required for stable film boiling.

Flow Boiling
In flow boiling, two-phase fluid is forced over the heating surface. Vertical thermosiphons and pump-through reboilers operate
in flow boiling. The same principles of boiling apply to pool and flow boiling. The major difference is that due to the two-phase
flow, usually occurring in a restricted channel like a tube, hydrodynamic effects are more pronounced in flow boiling. The
limitations on the hydrodynamic effects become even greater at low pressure. The maximum heat flux is limited not only by
transition to film boiling but also by mist flow and potential flow instability.
Mist flow occurs when the shear force of the vapor at the vapor-liquid interface is sufficient to strip the liquid film from the wall,
producing a dry wall condition. This can be prevented by keeping the exit vapor weight fraction (y ) less than 0.5. When the

VERY LOW TEMPERATURE DIFFERENCE
When process conditions or economics require the use of a very low ∆T (8°F, 4°C), the ability to produce bubbles may differ
drastically for different surfaces. This makes design, performance prediction, and operation difficult. One possible solution is to
use tube surfaces which enhance nucleate boiling. Special coatings and specially formed fins are commercially available as
described in Section IX-A.
In thermosiphons, a low temperature difference will suppress the pumping action and cause low circulation rates. For vertical
tubeside thermosiphons, it is recommended to maintain a minimum heat flux of 2000 Btu/hr ft2 (6000 W/m2) to provide good
circulation. At lower flux levels it is preferable to use a kettle type exchanger.
VERY HIGH TEMPERATURE DIFFERENCE
A transition flow regime and mist flow condition can occur in reboilers operating at a high temperature difference, leading to
local hot spots and thermal cycling; such operating conditions should be avoided. In the transition region, small shifts in
operating conditions can cause large changes in performance. Also, this region has reverse control characteristics. That is,
because of a mixture of nucleate and film boiling conditions an increase in ∆T may sometimes cause a decrease in the heat
transfer. If the reboiler must be designed for film boiling it should be designed to operate in the stable region and not in the
transition region.
FOULING
Design fouling factors have a major impact on the operation of reboilers, especially under clean start-up conditions. If a large
fouling factor is used, the unit may be so oversurfaced at start-up that the required ∆T will be too small for proper operation.
Also, if the design ∆T is applied at start-up it may cause excessively high heat fluxes resulting in film boiling and higher than
normal tube wall temperatures, which may promote fouling.
Realistic fouling factors suggested by HTRI are shown in the table below (also refer to Table 3, Section IX-B). Fouling can be
minimized by ensuring good flow distribution on the shell side. Some ways to prevent bad distribution are: use Double Split
Flow (TEMA type H) design, avoid large bypass areas, avoid stagnant zones. For fluids with high fouling tendencies, keeping
the vapor fraction below 0.2 also helps to minimize fouling.

HORIZONTAL THERMOSIPHON SHELLSIDE REBOILERS
Heat Transfer
The hand calculation method for heat transfer in horizontal shell side thermosiphon reboilers is the same as for kettle reboilers.
Additional convective effects due to the flow are usually small but can be significant at high flow rates. These effects are
neglected as a safety factor.
For horizontal thermosiphons the heat flux should be limited to 70% of the calculated maximum heat flux to ensure operation in
nucleate boiling, and the weight fraction vaporized should be in the range of 0.1 to 0.5 to prevent slug flow at lower weight
fractions and mist flow at higher weight fractions.

WASTE HEAT BOILERS
The following procedure is based on steam generation using conventional tubes (no extended surface).
Exchanger Orientation - The units can be installed in either a vertical or horizontal position, and can have one or more tube
passes. In general, one tube pass vertical units have been used most successfully in the past (in cat regenerator flue gas and
fixed bed reactor service). However, in recent years, one tube pass horizontal units have been used with increasing frequency
(e.g., reformer effluent waste heat boilers).
Shell Side or Tube Side Vaporization - Steam is normally generated in the shell (firetube design) for the following reasons:
1. The hot fluid is frequently so fouling that it must be passed through the tubes.
2. With clean boiler water, it is permissible to use a non-removable tube bundle (fixed tube sheet unit) with triangular tube
pitch. This type of construction results in a compact unit with a low initial cost.
3. There is less differential expansion between the tube and the shell.
Tube Selection
1. For installations that recover heat from cat regenerator flue gases or reformer effluent, the number of tubes is based on
maintaining an inlet gas velocity of 100 ft/sec (31 m/s). For cat regenerator flue gases use 2-1/2 in. (63.5 mm) O.D., 5
BWG tubes. For reformer effluent, the minimum nominal tube size is 1-1/2 in. (50.8 mm) O.D., 10 BWG. The length of
the tubes will vary for each installation, depending on the surface area required. If necessary, the tube length may be 25 ft
(7.6 m) or more (see Section IX-C).
2. Fixed-bed reactors for exothermic processes are often constructed as shell-and-tube exchangers. In these units, the
hydrocarbon passes through the tubes, which are filled with catalyst. Steam is generated on the shell side. For this
service, use 2-1/2 in. (63.5 mm) O.D., 5 BWG tubes. The number and length of tubes will vary for each installation,
depending on process requirements as well as on heat transfer requirements. Steam generator-reactors for
polymerization plants have been designed with 4 ft (1.2 m) I.D. shells, and 180 (9.1 m) thirty-foot-long tubes.
