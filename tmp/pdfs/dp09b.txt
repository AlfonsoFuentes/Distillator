GENERAL
Figure 1 outlines the logic for all types of heat exchange equipment design. It is an iterative procedure involving
thermodynamic, thermal, mechanical and economic considerations. The purpose of the following section is to introduce the
designer to the analytic methods used in the thermal design as well as some mechanical aspects of heat exchange equipment
design.
In addition, the Energy Management System (EMS) has Design Leading Pratices on instrumentation requirements and
cleaning considerations for the energy critical exchangers. Refer to the EMS leading practices books for these practices and
for a procedure to identify energy critical exchangers.

coefficient, the area of the surface, and the effective temperature difference between the hot and cold fluids:

7. Calculate U (see below) from the physical properties of the fluids, fouling factors and the exchanger geometry.
o

value of U there is an exchanger design that satisfies the criterion that A calculated equals A assumed. However, only a

few of these designs are reasonable.

OPERATING TEMPERATURES
The operating temperatures of the exchanger are usually set by process conditions. However, in certain cases, the exchanger
designer will establish the operating temperatures. In a typical refinery or petrochemical plant, exchangers may be operating at
temperatures as high as 1000°F (540°C) or as low as -200°F (-130°C). These limits are dictated by material considerations,
safety, economics and ASME Code requirements.
TEMPERATURE OF STREAMS TO STORAGE
The maximum (or sometimes minimum) temperature of a stream going to atmospheric storage generally is set by safety,
economics or special process conditions. See Section XV, Safety in Plant Design. See also Offsite DP Section XXII, Storage
Facilities.
Safety Considerations - The temperature of a stream going to an atmospheric tank at sea level should not exceed that
temperature at which its true vapor pressure is equal to the values listed below.

For heavy streams whose true vapor pressure is difficult to determine, the maximum temperature to tankage should be the
lower value of either 50°F (28°C) below the ASTM initial boiling point or 15°F (8°C) below the minimum flash point.
Streams should not be sent to tankage at temperatures above 200°F (90°C) for API 650 tanks, and below 250°F (120°C) for
hot tanks. Operation in the 200 to 250°F (90 to 120°C) temperature range could result in condensation when the tank cools
forming a water heel which could subsequently flash to steam, resulting in a boilover.
Economics - The selection of the optimum temperature of a stream going to cone-roof tankage is generally based on an
economic balance between the cost of incremental cooler surface and cooling water and the savings due to reduced vapor
losses. The method of determining vapor losses is given in the following API Bulletins:
• API Bulletin 25 - 16 (low pressure tanks)
• API Bulletin 25 - 17 (floating roof tanks)
• API Bulletin 25 - 18 (cone roof tanks)
• API Bulletin 25 - 19 (internal floating roof tanks)
Since the losses from a floating roof tank can be negligible, the optimum temperature is sometimes the maximum allowed by
safety considerations (consult the EETD Safety, Risk and Site Inspection Section).
Special Considerations - Opportunity for optimizing the temperature of a stream going to storage is greatest for intermediate
products. However, special considerations are required for the following cases.
1. Streams that are stored prior to a process requiring refrigeration of the feed.
2. Streams whose properties are permanently degraded by high storage temperatures.
3. Streams that are stored before blending. The storage temperatures for such streams should be chosen after considering
the properties and temperature of the blend, assuming no heat loss in intermediate tankage.
In addition to the limitations given above, the temperature of a stream going to final storage is normally limited to a maximum of
135°F (57°C) if the product is to be shipped by barge or tanker. However, temperatures up to 150°F (66°C) are sometimes
used, provided that the high-temperature operating procedure is approved by shipping personnel.

COOLING WATER TEMPERATURES
The maximum allowable cooling water outlet bulk (or “mixing-cup") temperatures, set by fouling considerations, for coolers
other than box coolers (or worm coolers) are:
• Salt water: 120°F (48°C)
• Brackish water: 125°F (51°C)
• Fresh water: 130°F (54°C)
The maximum temperature used for a project should be checked with the affiliate, since this has an important bearing on
materials selection.
Another important criterion is the maximum allowable cooling water outlet film temperature. The maximum film temperature is
the water temperature at the liquid-metal interface occurring at the hot end of the exchanger. A conservative estimate of this
film temperature is the tube metal temperature at the hot end of the exchanger. These limits are:
• Salt water: 140°F (60°C)
• Fresh water: 150°F (65°C)
Note: For box coolers, the maximum cooling water outlet temperature is 150°F (65°C) for both salt water and fresh water.
If water film temperatures are allowed to exceed these values, severe fouling and corrosion could result.
In those cases where the hot stream outlet temperature is equal to or lower than the maximum allowable cooling water outlet
temperature, a brief economic study may be required to set the optimum cooling water outlet temperature. The study would
compare material versus cooling water requirements for varying water outlet temperatures. However, the cooling water outlet
temperature is normally determined by designing the exchanger so that the log mean temperature difference correction factor
(F , discussed in the section titled EFFECTIVE TEMPERATURE DIFFERENCE) is equal to the minimum allowable value (0.8).
n
Keep in mind the possibility of using a two-shell pass unit or two shells in a series.
At times, a condenser or cooler might be designed that uses a large amount of cooling water with a relatively low outlet
temperature. Consideration should be given to reuse this water in other coolers where the water outlet temperature may be the
maximum permissible. Box coolers always operate with reused water.

EFFECTIVE TEMPERATURE DIFFERENCE
The driving force for heat transfer is the “effective temperature difference," MTD , between the hot and cold fluids. This
e
temperature difference is calculated from the counter-current log mean temperature difference with a correction factor applied
to account for the actual flow arrangement.
For exchangers where the flow of hot and cold fluids is truly counter-current or co-current, MTD is equal to the log mean
e
temperature difference, LMTD. A chart for calculating LMTD appears in Section IX-D. Almost all exchanger designs attempt to
achieve counter-current flow since this flow arrangement has the highest obtainable value of MTD for any given set of fluid
e
temperatures.
In most commercial exchangers, however, using multiple tube passes or shell baffles causes the fluid to flow partially counter-
current and partially co-current. Since co-current flow results in lower LMTDs, a correction factor must be applied to the
counter-current LMTD. This correction factor, called F , is a function of the particular exchanger design, the number of units in
n
series and stream temperatures.

TEMPERATURE APPROACH
Temperature approach is the difference of the hotside and coldside fluid temperatures at any point within a given exchanger.
In a counter-current flow heat exchanger, it is possible for the exit temperature of the cold fluid to be higher than the exit
temperature of the hot fluid. This condition is known as a temperature cross and is illustrated in Figures 2A through 2C. Three
cases are depicted:
Figure 2A: Temperature distribution in a purely co-current flow heat exchanger (i.e., double pipe exchanger) with no
temperature cross.
Figure 2B: Temperature distribution in a double pipe exchanger with a temperature cross.
Figure 2C: Temperature distribution in a shell and tube exchanger with a single shell-pass and two tube-passes with a
temperature cross.
A temperature cross indicates a negative driving force for heat transfer between the fluids. It requires either a large area for
heat transfer or high fluid velocities to increase the overall heat transfer coefficient.
If outlet temperatures form a cross in a multi-tubepass heat exchanger, a lower than desirable LMTD correction factor (F -
n
factor) will occur. A simple way to avoid this is to use more exchanger shells in series. To determine how many shells to use in
series, refer to Figure 2A through 2F in Section IX-D, with F = 0.8 criterion, or use the graphical method illustrated in Figure
min
2D of this section. An exchanger design with an F < 0.8 will have outlet temperatures that cross. These exchangers require a
n
large increase in surface area for a small increase in heat duty.
Although there is no definite value of minimum F -factor for design, an F -factor less than 0.75 should not be used. An
n n
engineering judgement must be made based on economic and technical factors. Adding more shells in series will increase cost
significantly, and the designer may choose to use a larger-size single unit with a slight temperature cross. A general guideline
for minimum F -factor, especially when designing new exchangers, is to use 0.8 which is a widely accepted engineering
n
practice.
For an economical design of exchangers, several factors should also be considered such as incremental area and shell costs,
overall heat transfer coefficient, fluid heat capacities, inlet temperature difference and the savings/investment ratio, etc. An
analysis based on these factors to determine temperature approach and number of shells is presented in Exxon Technical
Report EE.6E.83, Incremental Heat Flux Method for Heat Exchanger Optimization dated January 1983 by Ghamarian, Thomas
and Sideropoulos.
In some cases (existing exchangers), a small temperature cross may be tolerated [up to 5°F (-15°C)], as shown in Figure 2C.
However, for all new designs a temperature cross should be avoided if possible. A temperature cross might occur for an
oversurfaced or cleaned exchanger service since the outlet temperature will probably exceed the design operating
temperatures where there is no temperature control. The sharp drop of the F -factor (and of course the effective MTD), due to
n
closer temperature approach, will offset the extra surface of the exchanger.
Although the co-current flow arrangement (Figure 2A) does not allow any temperature cross and results in a lower MTD , it has
e
some unique characteristics:
1. Co-current flow produces a more uniform tube wall temperature.
2. The highest tube wall temperature is comparatively lower than that for counter-current flow.
3. Since the cold end of the hot stream meets the warmer end of the coolant medium, co-current flow is often used to avoid a
freezing problem of the warmer fluid (e.g., airfin coolers in pitch service).
4. Since the MTD is higher at the entrance region, it will initiate an earlier nucleate boiling in vaporizing service. The
possibility of excessive heat flux must be explored in this case (vapor blanketing).

HEAT INTEGRATION USING PINCH TECHNOLOGY
This section provides the procedures to evaluate heat integration and guide development of more efficient systems, both within
a given unit and across an entire site using pinch technology. Pinch technology is based on fundamental thermodynamic
principles. This section also provides a procedure to apply pinch technology to optimize heat integration and process-to-utility
heat exchange on capital projects.
APPLICABILITY
While heat recovery analysis using pinch technology can be completed for any process unit, Energy Management System
(EMS) rollouts will use one or more of the following criteria to select process units for analysis:
• Total thermal energy consumption is $3 M/yr or greater
• There are margin incentives due to heat input or recovery constraints
• There is close proximity to a unit with a potential of inter-unit heat integration
The capital element of EMS provides guidance on when it is relevant to apply this technology to capital projects. In this case, a
heat integration analysis should be performed as soon as the heat and material balance is available. The capital project could
be
• A grassroots unit
• Revamp with significant change in heat and material balance (e.g., when de-bottleneckiing or when installing or modifying
heat transfer networks)
Site-wide steam supply/demand profiling can be used in any of the following situations:
• When a site-wide energy survey/analysis is performed. For example, during an EMS site integration process (SIP)
analysis
• When an existing process unit with significant interaction with utilities undergoes major revamp
• When a new process unit with significant interaction with utilities is being installed at a site
• When the utility system undergoes a major revamp.
The Heat Transfer and Energy Section of ER&E should be contacted for the following advanced concepts not covered here:
• Steps to configure a grassroots heat exchanger network to meet energy targets
• Methods to modify the existing heat exchanger network to improve the energy utilization efficiency

HEAT RECOVERY ANALYSIS FOR A PROCESS UNIT
The heat recovery analysis on a process unit would calculate the ideal heat recovery target, compare it with the current usage
to determine the gap, identify specific inefficiencies, and develop alternatives to close the gap. The identified modifications are
checked to ensure the technical and economical feasibility. The procedure given below of applying heat recovery analysis
using pinch technology on individual process unit is used in conjunction with Figure 3.
Collect accurate and consistent data to perform a successful process heat integration analysis using pinch technology.
The data required for process units are
• Process Flow Diagram
• Prices for utilities and exchangers
• Furnaces and Boilers
• Process-process and process-utility exchangers
− Temperatures in and out of each exchanger
− Exchanger duty
− Existing exchanger area
The source of data could be
• Plant Measurement
• Simulation (HEXTRAN, PRO II or equivalent)
• Heat & Material Balance
The templates in Tables 6 - 9 can be used for organizing data collected from the plant.
Perform the following steps to calculate the heat recovery targets:

FOULING FACTORS
The increased resistance to both heat transfer and fluid flow caused by deposits on a heat transfer surface is called fouling.
The following categories of fouling have been recognized:
1. Precipitation Fouling - Most frequently observed as a crystallization on the heat transfer surface of a relatively insoluble
salt dissolved in a stream (e.g., calcium carbonate).
2. Particulate Fouling - Often seen as sedimentation where particles suspended in the fluid, such as sand or rust, deposit on
the heat transfer surface.
3. Chemical Reaction Fouling - Coking, the deposit of hydrocarbons on high temperature heat transfer surfaces, is the most
prevalent form observed in the refinery or chemical plant.
4. Corrosion Fouling - Deposits, such as iron oxide, formed by a chemical reaction involving the heat transfer surface.
5. Biological Fouling - The adherence and growth of organic organisms on heat transfer surfaces where untreated water is
present. An example is the formation of algae from sea water.
6. Freezing Fouling - The solidification of a liquid onto a heat transfer surface.
Combinations of these categories often exist.
Fouling works as an insulating layer on the heat transfer surface, reducing heat transfer efficiency (reduced duty) or decreasing
available flow area (reduced throughput). The increased resistance to heat transfer is represented by a quantity referred to as
the fouling thermal resistance, which is added to the total thermal resistance. The values of fouling thermal resistance have
generally been observed to increase with time. To account for the effect of fouling on pressure drop requires an estimate of the
fouling layer thickness.
For design purposes a maximum permissible or expected value of the time-varying thermal resistance is used. This quantity is
the fouling factor (denoted by the symbols r and r described in the beginning of the section). The net effect of the fouling
io o
factor is to increase the required amount of heat transfer surface area. At startup, the exchanger is oversized while just before
scheduled cleaning, it is barely adequate.
The assignment of fouling factors allows the designer to identify where fouling dominates in a heat exchanger (shell side or
tube side for a shell and tube exchanger). Fouling resistances can be compared to the other thermal resistances (such as film
resistances R and R ). Based on this comparison, one may be able to alter the design to reduce fouling. In addition, one can
io o
determine the amount of surface area allocated for fouling.
Fouling factors should not be used as safety factors to account for some uncertainties in the design.

Physical factors that affect fouling include:
1. Fluid Properties and the Tendency for Fouling - For example, the feed to hydroformers, catalytic reformers and catalytic
cracking plants is sometimes severely fouling due to organic reactions with oxygen that take place while the feed is in
intermediate tankage. Inert gas blanketing of the tankage is often used to reduce the fouling tendencies of the fluid.
2. Surface and Bulk Temperatures - Higher temperatures generally result in faster rates of fouling.
3. Fluid and Velocities - Higher velocities reduce fouling rates, while stagnant regions or “dead zones" lead to higher rates
of fouling. This is especially true for cooling water that contains salt, crude with sand, slurry solutions and dusty gases.
Shell side velocities are limited by flow-induced vibration while tube side velocities are restricted by erosion considerations.
4. Material Selection and Configuration - Selecting the proper tube material may strongly affect corrosion. Finned tubing
may not be appropriate for heavy fouling services. It has been found that algae growth on titanium surfaces in seawater
service is much less than that on copper alloys.
5. Exchanger Geometry and Orientation - The geometry of the exchanger can affect the distribution of tube side and shell
side flows. The orientation of the exchanger and pitch of the tubes can be altered for easy cleaning.
6. Mode of Heat Transfer - Fouling for a particular fluid may be significantly different depending on the mode of heat transfer
(single-phase heating or cooling, boiling or condensing). Vaporization, for example, can cause severe precipitation fouling
by concentrating the foulants in the remaining liquid to the point of supersaturation.
7. Fluid Purity and Treatment - Filtering streams to remove impurities and treating fluids (such as with additives) can
drastically alter fouling. Crude oils, which normally contain salt-bearing water and suspended solids, are often placed in a
desalter or a flash drum to remove water before the crude reaches the “salting-out" temperature.
8. Placement of the More Heavily Fouling Fluid - Cleaning of the tube side is generally easier than the shell side. Also,
there is less chance of stagnant or “dead zones" on the tube side. Therefore, the more heavily fouling fluid should be
placed on the tube side.
9. Cleaning Method and Schedules - The cleaning method (such as online mechanical cleaning, offline cleaning, bundle
removal or in-place) should be determined at the design stage. Access to the exchanger should be considered to allow
easy cleaning.
The best source of values for the fouling factor is plant data obtained from heat exchangers in similar services under similar
operating conditions. Often these data are not available and the designer must rely on the values in Tables 3-5. Tables 3-5
gives normal fouling factors for a variety of process services, as recommended by the Tubular Exchanger Manufacturers
Association (TEMA) and modified by experience. Note that the resistances given apply to the surface on which the fouling
occurs. Although it appears to be somewhat ridiculous to adjust a value of such crude precision to a reference area, this step is
required for computer calculations and is done during manual calculations for consistency. The tabulated fouling factors are
intended to protect the exchanger from delivering less than the required process heat load for a period of about a year to a year
and a half. However, this table is only a guide, and if site- (and unit-) specific data are available to determine a more accurate
fouling factor for a particular service, the data should be used in preference to the values in Tables 3-5.
The actual importance of the fouling factors depends on the value of the clean coefficient U ; the larger the coefficient, the
c
larger the effect of the fouling factor. After a preliminary calculation of U , it is easy to determine the effect of doubling (or
c
halving) the assumed fouling factors on the size of the exchangers. If this effect is small (5% or less), it is not worthwhile to try
to determine a more accurate fouling factor. There are many times, however, when U is so large that the size of the
c
exchanger depends almost entirely on the value of the fouling factor. For these cases, plant data, when available, should be
closely examined.
The precision of the fouling factors used in the design should be weighed against the importance of the exchanger in its
service. For example, one would want very precise fouling factors for an exchanger in a critical service. On the other hand,
less accurate fouling factors may suffice where backup exchangers or bypass lines are available.

PRESSURE DROP
The pressure drop through an exchanger is made up of three losses: the frictional loss due to flow, the losses due to changes
in direction of flow and losses due to expansion and contraction into and out of nozzles and tubes. In some exchangers, a
change in the vertical elevation of the fluid as it passes through the exchanger may cause a hydrostatic pressure loss or gain.
The method of calculating pressure drop is different for each type of exchanger and will be discussed under the appropriate
sections. Refer to Table 11 for typical exchanger pressure drop values.
The design of a heat exchanger is usually based on an economic balance between the cost of exchanger surface and the cost
of pumps or compressors. The cost of a utility such as water may often enter into the economic balance. High utility mass
velocities through the exchanger give a higher coefficient and a smaller surface area, but require a higher pressure drop, or an
increase in utility flow rates. This in turn results in an installation with higher than optimum investment and operating costs for
the pump or compressor and lower exchanger investment cost. Occasionally, a particular system may have excess pressure
drop available; in such cases, this pressure drop should be utilized to minimize the cost of the exchanger.
In general, the necessity for a detailed economic balance between pressure drop and exchanger surface can be determined
from looking at the effect of the individual film coefficient on the overall heat transfer coefficient. Little can be gained by
specifying increased pressure drop for one fluid in an exchanger when the other fluid has a significantly lower film coefficient.
For such cases, a detailed economic balance is unnecessary.
High pressure drops are not desirable if they contribute to erosion, require excessively thick pass partitions or other internals, or
result in uprating valves, fittings or piping in the heat exchanger circuit.

CYCLIC SERVICE
The design pressure, design temperature and CET of the hot and cold sides of an exchanger are determined independently.
The considerations outlined in Section II of this manual, Design Temperature, Pressure and Flange Ratings, should be followed

Occasionally, an exchanger is immediately followed in the circuit by a second exchanger (i.e., a cooler), with a bypass provided

If the shell side design pressure is quite low, the tube side (water) design pressure should be reduced, if possible, taking into

POSITIONING OF UNITS, NOZZLE LOCATIONS AND PIPING
POSITIONING
Normally, heat exchangers are positioned horizontally. They may be positioned vertically, however, for minimizing solids
deposition (such as in flue gas exchangers) and for vertical thermosiphon reboilers with boiling in the tubes.
NOZZLE LOCATION
The following rules are suggested as a guide for locating heat exchanger nozzles:
1. Streams being heated or vaporized should flow from bottom to top, whether on the tube side or the shell side.
2. Streams being condensed should flow from top to bottom, whether on the tube side or the shell side.
3. The direction of flow of streams being cooled should be dictated by piping economics.
4. For multi-tube-pass exchangers the last tube pass should be in counter current flow with the shellside fluid.
5. U-tube exchangers: Where possible, the shell inlet nozzle should be placed beyond the U-bend to avoid removing tubes to
allow for an impingement plate.

BYPASSES AND BLOCK VALVES
Bypasses and block valves are used on an exchanger for one or more of the following reasons:
PROCESS CONTROL
When it is necessary to control the heat transfer in an exchanger, use either:
1. A simple bypass valve and two block valves or
2. A bypass and a three-way valve (or two butterfly valves), an arrangement that splits the flow between the bypass and the
exchanger. See Section XII-F. A three-way valve is necessary when the exchanger pressure drop is so small that
insufficient fluid diversion would take place through a simple bypass in the wide open position.
In single phase services the set ups described above are most frequently used to control heat transfer, however, for two
phase services other methods of heat transfer control exist and can be used such as flooding the bundle and throttling a
steam inlet.
LEAKAGE
Where leaking of one side of a heat exchanger would result in intolerable contamination of the other fluid (as in an overhead
vapor-feed exchanger of a pipestill), blocks and bypasses may be installed to permit isolating the leaking unit from the system.
In addition, welded tube-to-tubesheet joints or double tubesheets should be considered.
MAINTENANCE
Blocks and bypasses may be installed on both sides of the exchanger to permit temporary removal of the unit for maintenance.
When the fouling rates are known and cleaning is the only maintenance consideration, the decision to use blocks and bypasses
depends on an economic balance between incremental exchanger surface (or cost of downtime) and the bypass facilities.
Exchanger tube bundles may be cleaned either in place or removed from the shell. When the fouling compound can be
removed by chemical action or solution, cleaning time and costs are reduced (refer to the Chemical Cleaning Manual for
further information). When chemical cleaning is to be employed, 1-1/2 in. (40 mm) chemical cleaning connections are required
(refer to GP 3-6-4 and GP 6-1-1). These connections will be provided on the exchanger nozzles.

CONDENSER LOCATION AT GRADE
The location of a condenser at grade lower than the distillate drum (“submerged" condenser) reduces the amount of structural
steel required and simplifies maintenance. However, for a constant distillate drum pressure, the submerging of a condenser
causes an increase in system pressure over that for elevated condensers because of the static head of fluid in the line
connecting the condenser with the distillate drum. This pressure increase results in a higher boiling temperature. An increased
boiling temperature may be significant in low pressure towers, affecting units handling materials subject to temperature
degradation and those with reboilers having a close temperature approach or operating close to critical temperature at tower
bottoms. Do not locate low-pressure [less than 20 psia (140 kPa)] condensers at grade.
Total Condensers - Some total condensers are located at grade when the increase in temperature due to submerging is not
objectionable. Each submerged shell and tube unit requires a 1 in. (25.4 mm) valved vent connection to the vapor space of the
distillate drum. This vent is used to bleed off any noncondensibles that might accumulate in the shell of the condenser and
blank off heat transfer surface.
If total condensers on a tower overhead system are elevated, a flooded bundle is used for pressure control.
Partial Condensers - Pressure elevation to avoid submergence is negligible for some partial condensers because of the low
effluent density. However, the line connecting the condenser to the distillate drum should be designed for “spray" or “annular"
two-phase flow (see Section XIV). Operation of a condenser at varying conditions resulting in differing outlet vapor rates can
give unstable flow conditions and pressure surges in the effluent line.
In systems where the condenser serves a distillation column, these surges are reflected in tower pressure and can result in
poor tower operation, especially with sieve trays or jet trays. For this reason, submerging of partial condensers is
recommended only for cases in which the condenser effluent rate and composition for all reasonably expected operations do
not vary to the extent that the flow in the condenser outlet line becomes unstable. For all other cases, a partial condenser
serving a fractionating tower should be elevated above the distillate drum.
Partial condensers in systems in which the vapors are generated by means other than a distillation tower, such as a reactor,
should be located at grade unless pressure surges from unstable flow conditions in the condenser effluent line would be
detrimental to the process.
DRUMLESS CONDENSERS

necessary for proper operation of such units. The design requirements affect the sizing of the condenser and the pump suction
line (refer to Section IX-F, Calculation Procedure, Condensation, for specific information pertaining to the design of these units).
